Activity recognition reads an accelerometer strapped to somebody's wrist and decides whether they were
walking, sitting or lying down. Commercial wearables need frequent recharging, which a study running for weeks
with ill or elderly participants cannot count on, and this paper says that requirement may even be unethical.
What the device computes is fixed in firmware before it ships, so deferring that choice means sending raw
acceleration and putting every sample on the radio.

A group of features costs less than the sum of its members. The standard deviation cannot be computed without
the mean, so a group holding both costs what the standard deviation alone costs, and everything computed on
jerk-transformed data shares that transform. Our energy model prices a group as the individual costs minus
what they share, where the usual proxy is a count of features.

We timed a C library on an ARM Cortex-M3 wearable, where one window of raw acceleration costs 94.38 µC. Held
to a tenth of that, the best groups beat the same random forest given every feature on two of the three
datasets, 0.875 against 0.833 on the smartphone data. The multi-objective swarm gives the best trade-off of
the four search methods, and the Pareto front for each dataset is in the paper.

Every charge figure belongs to one board, so a different radio or clock rewrites the table and leaves the
dependency graph alone. Selection also runs offline on pilot data, so a device holds one point on that front
rather than paying for the expensive features only where cheap ones are ambiguous. The oddest choice is the
interquartile range of the jerk magnitude squared, which appears in no published work we could find and is the
only feature to make the top ten in five of the six rankings by algorithm and by dataset. Its appeal is
presumably that it is the cheapest of the features to transmit, and no comparison against equally cheap
features was run to find out.
