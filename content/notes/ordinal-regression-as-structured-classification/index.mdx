Some labels have an order but no arithmetic. A clinician grading cognitive decline as mild, moderate
or severe puts severe further from mild than moderate, and nobody can say how much further. In our
experience most machine learning work on ordinal medical problems drops the ordering and fits an
ordinary classifier, whose one-hot targets make every wrong answer equally wrong, and penalising
distant mistakes instead means writing a cost matrix out of the distances the scale never defined.

This paper puts the ordering in the label. A grade out of K becomes K-1 bits, the k-th set whenever
k falls below the grade, so four out of seven is 111000 and three is 110000. A conditional random
field chained across those bits predicts them jointly, under a log likelihood that turns out to
track the number of bits in disagreement, which is the distance between the true grade and the
predicted one. Nobody writes the cost matrix down; the encoding implies it.

We compare StORM against ordered logit, nested binary classifiers and logistic regression, all four
linear in their parameters. On a ten-category spiral, drawn in two dimensions so the right answer is
visible, it reaches a mean absolute error of 0.112 against 2.474 for ordered logit. The benchmark
collections, the two Alzheimer's datasets where nothing separates the four significantly, and the
queries the encoding opens up are in the paper.

Not every sequence the chain can emit is a valid label, since a zero followed by a one has no
ordinal reading. Zeroing an edge potential would forbid them, and this paper follows a different
observation instead, that invalid predictions seem to coincide with outlying data. Whether a
structured label space can flag its own outliers is the open question, and the model is presumably
declining to commit on a point unlike anything in training. The paper records the work as ongoing.
