I watched [a video showing how the sound in a recording can reveal exactly where it was
captured](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UytYrhYLOKI).
The presenter began with a security-camera recording known only to have been captured on September 8 somewhere in the
continental United States. By matching the changing pitch of faint aircraft sounds to historical flights, he narrowed
the location to a shoreline and identified the camera in Google Street View.

I immediately wanted to implement the idea to see how well it worked. I first built the search in Python. It worked
well, so I ported it to TypeScript for the interactive figures here. In both versions I avoided flight-data APIs and
large historical datasets by generating the terrain, flight plans, microphones, and Doppler curves myself. Generating
the data gives me ground truth for the flight paths, microphone position, and recording time, so I can benchmark the
search's accuracy.

I adapted the approach in the video to my synthetic flights. For each candidate microphone position and recording time,
the flight tracks predict which aircraft should be audible, when their sound would arrive, and how each aircraft's pitch
would change. Those predictions give each place and time an acoustic fingerprint to compare with the generated
recording. A second plane on a different path can rule out places that fit one flyover by chance.

## Sound travel time

When we hear a plane, we hear sound that was emitted in the past. Knowing the plane's altitude, speed, and position lets
us work backwards from when the sound arrives to when it was emitted. Each ring below shows how far sound from an
earlier aircraft position has travelled; the microphone hears that sound when the ring reaches it.

## Aircraft Doppler curves

In the first figure, the plane moved along a straight horizontal line. Its sound fronts crowd together ahead of it and
spread apart behind it, changing the pitch we hear. The next figure lets the flight path bend across a two-dimensional
map, so a turn also changes how quickly the plane approaches or recedes from the microphone. Change the path, speed, or
microphone position, then use the time marker to compare the plane's position with the frequency curve.

<DopplerLab />

Let $f_0$ be the emitted tone and $c$ the speed of sound. At emission time $t_e$, the plane's speed directly away from
the microphone is $v_r(t_e)$. The received frequency is

$$
f_a = \frac{f_0}{1+v_r(t_e)/c}.
$$

A plane passing close to the microphone produces a rapid drop from high to low pitch; farther away, the drop is slower.
Turns and changes in speed add more detail to the curve. Those details help distinguish the curves predicted from
different flight paths and microphone positions[^flight-transfer].

The plane's emitted frequency is set in this demonstration. In a recording, that frequency is unknown, so the search
tests different values while matching the rise, fall, and timing of the received curve. It assumes the plane's tone
stays steady during a pass.

## Calculating the received curve

The received curve can be calculated in two ways. Starting at a time on the recording, we work backwards to find where
the plane was when it emitted that sound. Or we translate the flight path analytically: each emission time gives an
arrival time and a received frequency. The orange line is the analytical translation; the blue points are calculated
backwards from arrival times. They coincide. The microphone and flight-path points can be dragged on the map, and the
controls change the plane's speed, altitude, and emitted tone.

## Synthetic flights and recordings

For this setup, I generated random flight paths over a randomly generated terrain and placed microphones at known
locations and times. Because I know every plane's path, the sound-travel and Doppler equations tell me when each tone
reaches a microphone and how its pitch changes.

## Matching recordings to flight tracks

The search rejects poor matches between predicted and observed curves, then refines the best locations. Historical
flight tracks could come from aircraft position broadcasts known as ADS-B; this setup uses generated tracks instead.

The browser starts with clean Doppler curves rather than extracting them from audio. In the Python version, I also
generated audio and recovered the tonal curves from spectrograms before searching. Its synthetic recovery results were
broadly similar, though the two versions used different test cases. The full-day search also checks whether a candidate
would hear the same number of flights as the clip. This assumes every audible tone was detected, which is true for the
generated curves.

A microphone that hears only one plane gives limited information about its location, especially when other flights
follow similar paths. A second plane travelling in a different direction can narrow the possible locations. The

## Refining the location estimate

The location search above first checks positions across the full 20 × 20 km map. It then checks more closely spaced
positions around the eight best matches, repeating this three times before a final 25 × 25 search around the strongest
result. Reducing the initial spacing takes longer but makes it less likely that the correct area is discarded early. The
slider shows which areas were checked at each stage.

Over a larger area, the search could first exclude flights too far away to be heard. It could then refine the estimate
between tested points in the likely area.

## Searching a full day of flights

The final figure uses hundreds of flights across a 100 × 100 km terrain. It builds one fixed day, including two
deliberate passes near noon. Move the microphone and the flights stay put; some positions and hours yield too few tones
to search. The dashed circle around the cross shows the maximum tonal hearing range, while coloured aircraft dots leave
short trails in their direction of travel.

To try one run:

1. Click the map to place the microphone. Move the day clock to preview flights, then press **Set clip start** to choose
   the recording time.
2. Check which flights the microphone hears and press **Recover the listener**. The search receives the recording and
   the flight catalogue, but not the chosen place or time.
3. Move **Zoom through search levels** to inspect refinement. Select a ranked result to see its curves and a fine score
   grid over the terrain at that result's proposed time.

At each grid point, the search calculates when catalogue flights would be heard. It proposes a recording start when a
flight's arrival lines up with a tone in the clip; with multiple tones, it also checks the gap between arrivals. For
each proposed place and time, the score compares the predicted and recorded Doppler shapes and arrival times. It also
falls when that place and time predicts a different number of audible flights. The search then refines the
highest-scoring cells.

The five rows are the five best explanations of the recording, each a place and a time of day. Select one to rescore a

## Benchmark: 100 synthetic clips

Each of the 100 test clips is a 20-minute recording at a random microphone position and start time in the same 100 × 100
km day, with its 420 background flights and two constructed passes. I generated the curves that would reach each
microphone and gave them to the same TypeScript search that the figure uses. The true position and time were used to
generate the curves and measure error; the search did not receive either one.

The most thorough search used 1 km coarse cells, three refinement levels, and 80 cells kept at each level. A position
counts as recovered when it is within 200 m of the microphone. The clips have exact flight tracks and clean curves, so
this does not test audio detection or missing flights.

| Audible flights | Clips | Within 200 m | Mean position error | Median position error | Median start-time error |
| --------------- | ----: | -----------: | ------------------: | --------------------: | ----------------------: |
| 0               |    32 |  No estimate |                 n/a |                   n/a |                     n/a |
| 1               |    38 |            2 |             24.9 km |               18.5 km |                 4.3 min |
| 2               |    22 |           22 |                18 m |                  16 m |           Less than 1 s |
| 3 or more       |     8 |            8 |                21 m |                  19 m |           Less than 1 s |

The thorough search recovered all 30 clips with at least two audible flights, with a mean position error of 19 m. It
recovered only two of the 38 one-flight clips. In these clips, a finer search cannot separate places and times that fit
the same one-flight curve. The 32 clips with no audible flight produced no estimate.

I reran the 30 clips with at least two audible flights at three search settings. The interactive figure starts with the
quick 2 km, 20-cell search. The fine and thorough options take longer but keep more areas for refinement.

| Coarse spacing | Cells kept | Within 200 m | Mean position error | Mean search time |
| -------------- | ---------: | -----------: | ------------------: | ---------------: |
| 1 km           |         80 |     30 of 30 |                19 m |           25.3 s |
| 1 km           |         32 |     29 of 30 |               615 m |           21.4 s |
| 2 km           |         20 |     26 of 30 |              5.0 km |            6.8 s |

The lower settings can lose the answer before refinement starts. In one clip, the true place and time scored 100, but
the nearest point on the 1 km coarse grid scored only 25 and ranked 76th. Keeping 32 cells discarded the correct area.
Adding a fourth refinement level could not bring it back; keeping 80 cells preserved it.

The video uses a 200 m grid for candidate positions, then 100 m spacing for detailed curve scoring. This benchmark
starts at 1 km and refines selected areas three times, reaching about 37 m spacing. That finer final grid helps only if
the correct area survives the first pass, which is why the number of cells kept for refinement matters. The accuracy
here is an optimistic ceiling for this constructed setup: the generated curves have no ground reflections, changing air
conditions, noisy surroundings, or missed tones. Those effects can obscure or distort a real Doppler trace.

## How many flights are enough?

The chance of hearing at least two flights depends on flight rate and recording length. Suppose audible passes follow a
Poisson process: they arrive independently at a constant average rate of $\lambda$ per hour. Let $N(T)$ be the number of
audible passes in a recording $T$ hours long. Its expected value is $\lambda T$, and the chance of hearing at least two
is

$$
P(N(T)\ge 2)=1-e^{-\lambda T}(1+\lambda T).
$$

The 100 clips held 107 audible flights, an average of 1.07 per 20-minute clip. Putting that average into the expression
gives about 29% of clips with at least two flights, close to the 30% observed. That calculation describes only these
synthetic clips; I have not checked whether their flight arrivals follow a Poisson process. Real flights also cluster
around airports and schedules, so the arrival rate alone cannot predict how well recovery will work.

## Conclusion

In this synthetic setup, the method located microphones accurately when at least two flights were audible. The thorough
search recovered all 30 such clips in my 100-clip benchmark, usually within a few tens of metres. A single flight was
rarely enough, and a coarse search could still discard the right area before refinement. Reflections, noise, and missed
tones will make real recordings harder, so these results are an optimistic ceiling rather than a prediction for field
recordings. Even so, I am impressed by how much location information the curves from multiple flights can carry.

[^flight-transfer]:
    For a spline flight path $p(t)$ and microphone $o$, let $r(t)=\lVert p(t)-o\rVert$. Its radial speed is
    $r'(t)=(p(t)-o)\cdot p'(t)/r(t)$. Each emission time $t$ maps directly to the pair
    $\bigl(t+r(t)/c,\; f_0/[1+r'(t)/c]\bigr)$: arrival time and received frequency. For a subsonic flight, arrival time
    increases with emission time, so these pairs trace the heard curve without solving backwards at every audio sample.
    The heard curve is smooth on each spline segment. Because $r(t)$ contains a square root, the curve is generally not
    an exact polynomial spline. A compact spline can still approximate it to a chosen accuracy. Modeled amplitude varies
    smoothly with range too, while real signal-to-noise ratio also depends on background sound and obstructions. Only
    microphones within a flight's hearing range need its curve, so each grid cell can keep a short list of nearby flight
    fingerprints.
