A model that reads a sensor rarely reads the raw signal. The readings pass through a fixed chain of
arithmetic that summarises them into features a domain expert can interpret and criticise. The chain
costs nothing on the machine where the analysis was designed, and on the battery-powered node that
took the reading it can cost as much as everything else the device does. Learning a representation
from raw data removes the cost and the interpretation together.

The representation does not need learning, because it already exists. Filtering, windowing, a Fourier
transform, the spectral density and a logarithmically binned histogram are each deterministic, so
their composition is a fixed function of the raw window, and we regress a two hidden layer network
from that window onto the exact feature values. The output is the histogram itself rather than a
learned code, and the saving comes from imitating the whole chain at once instead of any one step.

We swept six network sizes against exact computation on accelerometer data from the SPHERE
Challenge. Feature error rose as the networks shrank, from a mean absolute error of 0.165 to 0.867
against targets spanning roughly 1.5 to 330, and the features arrived 2.4 to 22 times faster. Across
all six, a logistic regression trained on the approximations stayed within 0.04 of a percentage point
of the same classifier on exact features. The spectrograms and error distributions are in the paper.

All of it rests on the classifier not reading whatever the approximation lost. Knowing which feature
chains tolerate that, instead of fitting a network and measuring, is the open question. The errors
presumably fall in directions a decision boundary was already ignoring, since a boundary uses the
ordering and separation among features rather than their magnitudes, and that stays conjecture while
logistic regression is the only classifier here.
