Recipe recommendation puts the right dish in front of somebody, given what they have cooked and clicked on
before. Most methods for it read a recipe against a hand-labelled dictionary of canonical ingredients, tools
and skills, and have only been evaluated on one language at a time. Cookpad publishes in Arabic, English,
Indonesian, Russian and Spanish, and a dictionary maintained by hand describes exactly one of them, so a
second language means building the whole thing again.

The dictionary can be read off the recipes themselves. For each language we split raw ingredient text on
spaces, throw away two hundred quantity terms and anything carrying punctuation or a number, and keep the
fifteen hundred commonest strings. Cooking skills come from the same text as ingredient and verb pairs whose
mutual information clears a threshold set by how indiscriminate each half is. Neither representation needs a
dictionary written by hand.

Native speakers scored the normaliser against a system built on the platform's professionally maintained
proprietary dictionary. The unsupervised version had the lower error rate in all five languages, from 0.14 to
0.05 in Russian, without a labelled example in any of them. Bayesian optimisation chose the model family and
representation together and picked ingredients, skills and a hybrid model in every language, and on search
re-ranking the personalisation model beat the click-based cold-start baseline by around 20% on mAP@1. The
per-language tables are in the paper.

Text embeddings of the whole recipe need no dictionary either, and the optimiser never picked one. Every
personalisation model here is linear, so whether a targeted representation still beats general text under a
non-linear recommender is the open question. Our reading is that sub-word units averaged over a long recipe
converge on the average recipe of its cuisine and stop separating dishes that differ in one ingredient, which
the optimiser never picking one is at least consistent with.
