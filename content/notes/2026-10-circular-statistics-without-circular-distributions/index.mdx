Say you want to model when somebody does something during the day. The readings are clock times, so a normal fitted
to them treats a bedtime at 23:50 and one at 00:10 as twenty-three hours and forty minutes apart, even though they
are only twenty minutes apart. Any model of a daily routine needs some way to say that times near midnight are near
each other.

Circular statistics and wrapped distributions are the usual ways to approach these problems. Take a normal on the
line and wrap it round a circle of period $\gamma$, so its density is $\sum_k N(x;\mu+k\gamma,\sigma^2)$ summed over
every integer $k$. We fitted mixtures of these to smart home activity times in 2015 <Cite id="diethe2015circular" />.
They are awkward. The density is an infinite sum that has to be truncated, and in a mixture the components are not
pinned down, so a fit can wander round the clock without settling on anything.

<Ref id="nunezantonio2014bayesian" /> dealt with that by conditioning each point on which copy it came from. We took a
simpler route: approximate the wrapped normal by a mixture of ordinary normals with means one period apart, sharing a
variance, with the central mean held inside $[0,\gamma)$. That turned a wrapped mixture into an ordinary one, which
Gibbs sampling and expectation propagation could both fit.

The idea in this note is that you do not need the wrapped distribution at all, and you do not need to move the means
either. A normal depends on $x$ and $\mu$ only through $x-\mu$, so

$$
N(x;\mu+k\gamma,\sigma^2) = N(x-k\gamma;\mu,\sigma^2),
$$

term by term. Moving the mean up a period and moving the reading down a period are the same arithmetic. In our paper
we moved the mean. The other option is what this note does: leave one ordinary normal where it is, copy each reading
a day early and a day late, and score the three copies together. Wrapping stops being something the distribution does
and becomes something the loss does.

This is useful because a loss is easier to reuse than a distribution. Any model that gives you a density for a
periodic target, an hour of the day or a compass bearing, can be trained this way without changing the model: score
the target at $k\gamma$ offsets, take a log-sum-exp, and keep the predicted mean inside one period.

## How many copies you need

Three copies approximate the true density very accurately, as long as $\sigma$ is small compared with the period.
With $x$ and $\mu$ both inside $[0,\gamma)$ the nearest copy is never more than half a period from the mean and every
copy dropped is at least a whole period away, so writing $a=\gamma^2/2\sigma^2$, the density you compute falls short
of the true one by a relative error of at most

$$
\varepsilon \;\le\; \frac{2e^{-3a/4}}{1-e^{-3a}}.
$$

At the three-hour scale fitted below that is $8\times10^{-11}$, which is nothing. It is still under $0.005$ nats at
$\sigma=6$, a quarter of a day. By $\sigma=12$ it is about $0.4$ nats and three copies are no longer enough.

The paper answers the same question the other way round. It also writes the wrapped normal as a truncated Fourier
series, $\frac{1}{\gamma}(1+2\sum_n e^{-\sigma^2(2\pi n/\gamma)^2/2}\cos(2\pi n(x-\mu)/\gamma))$, and switches to that
form as $\sigma$ grows. Poisson summation turns one series into the other, and their rates,
$e^{-\gamma^2/2\sigma^2}$ against $e^{-2\pi^2\sigma^2/\gamma^2}$, are equal at $\sigma=\gamma/\sqrt{2\pi}$, about ten
hours on a day. Below that you need fewer copies, above it fewer harmonics, and a distribution that wide is close to
uniform anyway.

## Fitting it

Does it work? I compared three ways of fitting the same data: a conventional Gaussian mixture that ignores the
circle, the paper's version that shifts the means, and shifting the data.

For the last two, each point contributes the log of the sum over its copies, which is a log-sum-exp over three terms.
Nothing else in the training loop changes.

The setup is the same for all three options. Here I use Adam rather than the paper's Bayesian inference, which is
the same thing done a different way. One generating density is a single normal at 23:00 with $\sigma$ of three hours,
straddling midnight. The other is a mixture of two, weights 0.6 and 0.4, means at 22 and 9 hours, scales 2.5 and 2.0.
Each fit saw 500 draws and started with the means spread evenly round the circle, as the paper does.

<Explorer />

Shifting the data and shifting the means give the same fit. They agreed to $9\times10^{-16}$ in the loss at random
parameters and to $4\times10^{-15}$ in the fitted parameters across 40 fits. They are equivalent.

One detail is worth getting right. The copies go into a single log-sum-exp, not into the dataset as extra rows.
Adding rows scores each copy separately, so it maximises $\sum_k\log p(x-k\gamma)$ rather than $\log\sum_k
p(x-k\gamma)$, and that loss has to make every copy likely at once: on the single normal it fits $\sigma$ at 21.7
hours against a true 3.

## More than one circle

The same thing works in more dimensions. With $d$ periodic coordinates the copies are a grid rather than a row, so
each point is scored at $(2K+1)^d$ of them: nine in two dimensions keeping one copy either way, twenty-seven in
three. Polynomial in the copies kept per axis and exponential in the number of axes that wrap, which is what decides
where the idea stops being worth it.

<Torus />

Drag a component into a corner and its mass comes out of all four. The fit follows it round. Turn on the copies to
see what is being scored: the data in nine tiles, one model in the middle.

The model never changed through any of this: it is the same normal fitted with the same optimiser, and all that
changed is how the data is measured against it. None of which is better than what we did in the paper, and it could
not be, since the two give the same fit. It is a reinterpretation of it, and from a certain point of view a simpler
one: the awkward object goes away and what is left is a distribution anybody can write down.

## Takeaways

If your target is periodic, you do not need a periodic distribution. Keep the one you have, evaluate it at the
reading and at a period either side of it, and add those three densities together before taking the log. The model's
mean stays inside one period.
