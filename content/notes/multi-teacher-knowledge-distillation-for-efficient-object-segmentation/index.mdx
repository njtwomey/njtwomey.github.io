Distillation trades accuracy for speed. A small encoder trained to imitate SAM2's large one runs 3.2
times faster end to end and scores lower for it, and the obvious way to recover the accuracy is to
grow the student, which returns less speedup the larger it gets.

There is a cheaper source of accuracy, and it comes from the fact that a teacher only exists during
the training run. Once training finishes the teacher is discarded, so the student's parameter count
and its latency are identical whether one model supervised it or five did. Anything a teacher
contributes is free at inference, in a way that a wider backbone or a higher input resolution never
is.

Distilling from several foundation teachers rather than one turns out to be worth 2.3 mIoU on COCO
and 1.5 on LVIS, at exactly the same deployed cost. Whether that justifies the extra training
complexity depends on what consumes the masks, and the video results and ablations that bear on it
are in the paper.

The mechanism is presumably diversity. Teachers built differently make different errors, so a target
assembled from several of them sits closer to the truth on average than any one of them does, and
the student inherits fewer of any single teacher's blind spots. That is a conjecture rather than a
finding, since the experiments measure the outcome; separating the two would mean checking whether
the student's errors track the places where the teachers disagree.
