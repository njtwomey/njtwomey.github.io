Time series anomaly detection watches streams of measurements and flags the points that stop fitting
the pattern. Some of them are dense enough that a break in the rhythm is obvious, and the sparse
ones are the long tail of a large platform rather than its exceptions. No events in an hour is what
an outage looks like, and it is equally what an ordinary quiet hour looks like.

The limit belongs to the data rather than the detector. The counts are Poisson draws around a
seasonal rate, and a Poisson signal rises above its own noise only as the square root of that rate,
so normal and broken distributions slide into one another as a stream thins. Below some level of
traffic the anomaly signature is provably undetectable, and a classifier handed perfect knowledge of
the generating process traces that ceiling across count level and severity.

Measured against it, five widely used families all fall away as traffic drops: LSTM forecasting,
matrix profile, autoencoders, ECOD and isolation forest. Forecasting is the strongest of them on
dense streams and approaches random once the count level falls below $2^6$. Smoothing the raw
anomaly scores with an exponential moving average improved area under the precision-recall curve in
most settings and harmed it in none. The time-to-detection trade-off and a replication on Walmart
sales are in the paper.

Smoothing needs a decay constant, and ours came from how long an anomaly lasts in the simulation,
which a production monitor does not know. Choosing it without that number is what a production
monitor is left with, and a sweep against anomaly duration would answer it. The score is itself
noisy wherever counts are low, so a constant of roughly the right order should keep most of the
benefit.
