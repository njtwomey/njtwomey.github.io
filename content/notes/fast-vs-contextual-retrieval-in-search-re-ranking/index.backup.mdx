Search has had the same shape for a long time: a cheap stage sweeps millions of documents and returns a few
hundred candidates, and an expensive stage reads that shortlist carefully and puts it in order. The split
exists because the scoring function that ranks best is far too expensive to run over a whole corpus. BERT-base
scoring a thousand candidates took 10.7 s against 61 ms for a cheaper
scorer <Ref id="khattab2020colbert" paren />. I worked on ranking for recipe search a few years
ago <Ref id="takiguchi2021multifield" paren /> and have not kept up since. A friend asked me over dinner how
re-ranking had changed, and I did not have an answer. I knew the retrievers had been getting stronger, but not
what that had done to the overall picture. I looked into it out of curiosity, and this note is what came of
it. The two stages are still chosen separately, but the better the first stage, the less the second stage adds
and the shorter the shortlist it needs.

That pattern is already in the literature. <Ref id="meng2024ranked" /> held the re-ranker fixed and swapped
only the first stage, and the depth at which re-ranking stopped helping moved with it. Behind `BM25` quality
was still climbing at a thousand candidates, and behind a strong learned sparse retriever it peaked at twenty
candidates. How deep to re-rank is a property of the retriever in front rather than a setting on the
re-ranker.

I wanted to see how generally that holds, so I swept candidate depth on
SciFact <Ref id="wadden2020fact" paren />. It is a claim-verification set of 300 judged queries over 5,183 abstracts,
small enough to sweep from end to end. `all-MiniLM-L6-v2`, `BM25` and `bge-small-en-v1.5` retrieved the
candidates, and one cross-encoder, `ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2`, re-ranked the output of all three. Holding the
second stage fixed is what attributes a change to the retriever. Every number here is nDCG@10 over the 300
queries.

<DepthPlot />

At a thousand candidates the three pipelines land within 0.0007 of each other, at 0.683. By then the re-ranker
has re-scored nearly everything any of the three retrievers would have returned, so the ranking is entirely
its own. That number is what the re-ranker scores once the retriever has stopped constraining it, so a
retriever whose own score sits below it gains from depth and one above it loses. `all-MiniLM-L6-v2` at 0.645
gains, `BM25` at 0.676 sits close enough to do neither, and `bge-small-en-v1.5` at 0.713 loses. Behind the
strongest retriever of the three, re-ranking made things worse rather than better. The 0.030 it costs at the
deepest setting is the only movement behind that retriever whose interval excludes zero.

On the `bge-small-en-v1.5` arm the solid line never reaches its own dashed baseline, so re-ranking buys
nothing at any depth and the second stage can come out. That leaves a dense retriever serving its own ranking.
It pays a one-off cost to vectorise every document, and at query time it vectorises the query and looks for
the nearest of them, so the shape of the thing is much the same. Their properties differ, though. `BM25`
tokenises "curry, but not too spicy", drops the stopwords and searches for "curry spicy", roughly the opposite
of the request. The encoder reads the string in sequence, so "not too spicy" arrives as a phrase and the
vector carries the qualification rather than losing it.

Retrieval and re-ranking are trained, benchmarked and swapped independently, which is reasonable enough given
that they have historically been separate models built by separate teams, but the output of the system is not
independent. A hundred candidates is an inherited default rather than a measured one. On this collection it is
roughly right behind `BM25`, three times deeper than `all-MiniLM-L6-v2` needs, and past the point of harm
behind `bge-small-en-v1.5`. Upgrading the retriever here removed the case for the second stage rather than
shortening the shortlist. The two stages can be trained apart, but they have to be configured and measured
together.
